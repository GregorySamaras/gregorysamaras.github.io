AI Assistants Will Expose Your Data Governance Problems

AI doesn't just make information easier to find. It can expose years of overlooked decisions about who should have access to it.

Over time, every organisation accumulates permission debt. SharePoint sites without clear ownership, abandoned Teams channels, shared mailboxes with outdated permissions and security groups that haven't been reviewed in years.

These issues can go unnoticed because finding information still requires effort. Someone needs to know where to look, which system holds it and sometimes whether it exists at all.

An AI assistant can answer a question using documents, conversations and other business information without requiring the employee to search each source. That is useful, but it changes how easily existing access can be exercised. An employee might technically be allowed to open dozens of documents that they would never think to read together. Does that mean the organisation intended those documents to be combined into a single answer?

Many enterprise assistants work within user permissions, but permissions are not always a reliable reflection of who should have access. Connectors and integrations can introduce additional routes to data, depending on how they are configured.

New AI policies have their place. They cannot compensate for information with no clear owner, outdated access groups or retention rules nobody has revisited. Those are governance decisions organisations should already have made.

Before giving an assistant broad access to company information, I'd want to know whether the access it inherits still reflects deliberate choices. Otherwise, AI may be the first thing to reveal just how much permission debt has accumulated.
