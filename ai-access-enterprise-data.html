AI Assistants Will Expose Your Data Governance Problems

May 2026 · Opinion

Most organisations approaching enterprise AI are treating it as a technology adoption exercise.

I suspect it is actually an information governance exercise that happens to involve technology.

The reason is simple. AI does not create new access paths to information. It dramatically reduces the cost of discovering information that was already accessible.

The permission debt nobody wants to discuss

Every organisation accumulates permission debt.

SharePoint sites that nobody owns. Teams channels created for projects that ended years ago. Shared mailboxes with unclear access models. Security groups that were expanded temporarily and never reviewed. Folders inherited through multiple layers of nesting until nobody can confidently explain who has access to what.

Most of the time these issues remain hidden because discovering information still requires effort. Users need to know where to look, which system contains the information, and often whether the information exists at all.

AI changes that equation.

Search was never the real challenge

Enterprise search has existed for years. Organisations already possess vast quantities of searchable information across Microsoft 365, collaboration platforms, file shares, business applications, and cloud services.

What limited access in practice was friction.

Finding information required navigation, context, persistence, and familiarity with internal systems. Valuable information often remained undiscovered not because it was protected, but because it was difficult to locate.

AI assistants remove much of that friction. The user no longer needs to understand where information resides. They simply describe what they want and receive a response synthesised across multiple systems.

From a productivity perspective this is enormously valuable. From a governance perspective it changes everything.

Permissions are necessary but not sufficient

Security discussions around enterprise AI often focus on permissions.

If the user already has access to the information, what is the problem?

Technically, that argument is correct. Practically, it ignores how information is consumed.

Information that previously existed across separate systems, documents, conversations, and business processes can now be aggregated, summarised, prioritised, and contextualised automatically.

The question is no longer whether access exists. The question is whether the organisation intended that information to be combined in that manner and presented with that level of visibility.

The governance problem disguised as an AI problem

Many organisations will respond by creating AI policies, AI steering committees, and AI governance programmes.

Some of this is necessary.

However, I suspect many of the problems being classified as AI governance challenges are actually unresolved information governance challenges.

Data ownership. Classification. Retention. Access reviews. Accountability. Lifecycle management. These disciplines existed long before generative AI appeared.

The difference is that weak governance structures become far more visible when AI systems begin operating across organisational knowledge at scale.

The organisations that will benefit most

The organisations that gain the most value from enterprise AI are unlikely to be those deploying the most sophisticated models.

They will be the organisations that understand their information assets, maintain clear ownership, operate mature access control models, and possess confidence in the quality of their data.

In those environments, AI becomes an accelerator.

In poorly governed environments, it becomes an amplifier.

The distinction matters.

A familiar lesson in a new form

Throughout the history of enterprise technology, organisations have repeatedly discovered that automation magnifies existing strengths and weaknesses.

Cloud computing exposed weaknesses in infrastructure governance. Identity platforms exposed weaknesses in access management. Large-scale analytics exposed weaknesses in data quality.

Enterprise AI will do the same for information governance.

Organisations that view AI primarily as a technology project may find themselves solving governance problems they did not realise existed. Organisations that address those governance challenges first are likely to extract significantly more value from the technology.

AI is not creating a new information problem. It is accelerating an old one.
