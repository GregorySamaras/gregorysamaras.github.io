When Privacy Rules Shape Competition

An AI platform that analyses sales emails, identifies opportunities and helps employees respond to customers faster sounds like a reasonable investment.

But adopting the same technology can be a very different experience depending on where a business operates.

A company in the US might be able to introduce it relatively quickly. Businesses in the UK and EU must consider their respective GDPR requirements. In Germany, the same project could also require works council (Betriebsrat) involvement, particularly if the technology is capable of monitoring employee activity.

The technology hasn't changed. What changes is how much information it can access, what it can do with that information and the conditions under which it can operate.

Now consider two competing sales teams. One can use AI to analyse customer communications, identify opportunities and automate parts of its workflow. The other faces restrictions on what information the same technology can process.

Could that difference eventually influence their productivity and ability to compete?

And what happens when similar differences exist across thousands of businesses operating in different countries?

These restrictions exist for good reasons. An AI system analysing employee communications could also reveal individual performance, working habits and behaviour. But the consequences of different rules may extend beyond privacy.

As AI becomes more deeply integrated into business operations, the question of what data it can access may become just as important as what the technology itself can do.

We tend to see these decisions as matters of privacy and compliance. But perhaps we should also consider how they influence competition, investment and the wider economic landscape.
